How Sarasota Homeowners Can Extend HVAC Life

Routine maintenance is the difference between a system that lasts and one that breaks down in the middle of the season. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a new filter every one to three months, at least two feet of open space around the outdoor condenser, a spring rinse of the condenser coil, and two professional tune-ups a year, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Equipment age matters just as much. With regular service, central air typically lasts 12 to 15 years, a furnace 15 to 20, and a heat pump 10 to 15. Once a system passes that range and starts acting up, like higher energy bills, rooms that never reach the setpoint, or one repair after another, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule costs less and causes far less stress than scrambling for a new system during the first heat wave of summer.

The earlier you report a comfort problem, the cheaper it usually is. A weak capacitor is an easy, low-cost part to replace, yet that same capacitor, ignored through a full season, can take out the compressor and force a full system replacement. Common HVAC Problems in Sarasota

HVAC systems in this area face a familiar set of problems. Long cooling seasons push compressors and capacitors to their limits, and humidity and airborne grit slowly foul outdoor condenser coils, cutting efficiency years before the equipment should show its age. Clogged filters quietly choke off airflow, freezing evaporator coils in summer and overheating furnace heat exchangers in winter, damage that builds slowly and out of sight.

Our technicians also run into the full range of component failures in local homes: weak capacitors that leave the condenser humming without starting, contactors with pitted points, cracked igniters and dirty flame sensors that lock furnaces out on cold mornings, slow refrigerant leaks that leave the house lukewarm by afternoon, and blower motors worn down by years of restricted airflow. The area's older housing stock adds its own layer, undersized returns, leaky or crushed ductwork, and additions that no duct run ever reached.
